## Cycore plugins

### Get acquainted with the assorted Cycore plugin effects and see how this professional-grade bundle can lift the quality of your After Effects work.

Bundled inside Adobe After Effects is a collection of effects drawn from the Cycore (CC) plugin family, which most people simply call CC effects. They ship as part of the application itself, so they are already installed and woven into After Effects when you open it.

The purpose of CC effects is to give your projects an expanded palette of inventive visual treatments. The range stretches across multiple groups — 3D simulation, stylized treatments, motion graphics, and beyond. Reaching for CC effects lets you raise a project's visual impact and, at the same time, cut down on how complicated it has to be. You tune every effect from the Effect Controls panel, so dialing one in to fit your shot is straightforward.

Tip

- Open the Effects & Presets panel and type the keyword 'CC' into the search to surface the full set of Cycore plugin effects.
- Beside each effect in the Effects & Presets panel sits an information icon; click it to read more about that effect.

### Blur & Sharpen

What Blur & Sharpen effects control is how crisp or soft a picture or video reads. Apply them to a single layer, a clip, or an entire composition, whether you are correcting a focus issue or reaching for a stylistic flourish.

Reach for blur when you want to smooth out an image, suggest movement (motion blur, for instance), or push a background back so the subject reads more clearly. Sharpen does the opposite, pulling edges and fine detail forward.

The Effects panel is where you pick from the assorted Blur & Sharpen options, and every one of them carries its own collection of tweakable settings tailored to whatever your composition calls for.

- CC Cross Blur: Softens the image across both the horizontal and the vertical axes, handy when footage needs smoothing.
- CC Radial Blur: Smears the picture along the path of motion, opening the door to striking artistic looks.
- CC Radial Fast Blur: A speed-tuned blur that resolves more quickly than CC Radial Blur.
- CC Vector Blur: Smears the picture along the path of motion, opening the door to striking artistic looks.

### Channel

Sitting among the Channel effects, CC Composite gives you more latitude when blending one layer into another. It earns its keep on multi-layer work, since it governs how those layers read together — think masks and transitions, or simply how they visually combine.

Pictured: the CC Composite effect on a text layer, where the mask it makes uncovers the comp's bottommost layer.

### Color Correction

Tucked under Color Correction are effects for tuning the hues and tonal values in your footage. Use them to repair color trouble, push contrast, recolor, or align grades.

- CC Color Neutralizer: Tunes a layer's red, green, and blue independently so you can correct or restyle its overall color.
- CC Color Offset: Slides the red, green, and blue channels apart to produce eye-catching color shifts or distortions.
- CC Kernel: Reworks how each pixel relates to its neighbors, yielding looks such as blur or pixelation.
- CC Toner: Tints a layer by reworking its shadows, midtones, and highlights.

Pictured: the CC Color Offset effect on a layer with Red Phase nudged to play up the reds, warming the overall tone of the video image.

### Distort

With the Distortion effects you can remold layers through approaches like smearing, twisting, and stretching. Their controls hand you ways to recast how footage looks and arrive at one-of-a-kind visual outcomes.

- CC Bend It: Bows a layer between a pair of set points, echoing the way a rod or stick flexes.
- CC Bender: Arcs a layer around its vertical axis from one control point, producing a smooth bow.
- CC Blobbylize: Reworks a layer so it reads soft and fluid, as if it were jelly or liquid.
- CC Flo Motion: Warps a layer smoothly across a mesh driven by two clusters of control points.
- CC Griddler: Slices the layer into a grid-style pattern.
- CC Lens: Bends the layer outward from a center point to fake a fisheye lens.
- CC Page Turn: Plays back a believable page curl or flip, the way a sheet of paper turns.
- CC Power Pin: Pins a layer at all four corners so you can stretch it and reshape its perspective.
- CC Ripple Pulse: Sends concentric ripple rings out from the middle, the way a dropped object disturbs water.
- CC Slant: Tilts the layer on a diagonal toward whichever direction you pick for a leaning look.
- CC Smear: Pulls or drags a portion of the layer between two points for a smeared result.
- CC Split: Cuts the layer in half and sends the two pieces off in opposing directions.
- CC Split 2: A beefed-up CC Split that throws in extra knobs such as angle and edge softness.
- CC Tiler: Tiles the layer across the comp to lay down a repeating pattern.

### Generate

The CC Generate effects come into play when you need to manufacture visual pieces from nothing — textures, gradients, blocks of color, and similar elements.

- CC Glue Gun: Imitates how hot glue behaves as it lays down and sets, leaning into looks like dripping, flowing, and trailing.
- CC Light Burst 2.5: Has elements throw out a burst of light from one point, akin to a flash or a lens flare.
- CC Light Rays: Sends shafts of light fanning out from a source the way sunlight or a spotlight would.
- CC Light Sweep: Slides a glinting streak of light over surfaces, well suited to drawing the eye to text or objects.
- CC Threads: Spins up twisting, flowing strands or fibers, a natural fit for abstract imagery and lively motion design.

### Keying

Got an unwanted element in your shot — a wire or some small object? The Simple Wire Removal effect is built to take it out. Its controls let you paint across the offending item so it melds into what surrounds it. The effect pays off most on locked-off shots, where that object holds the same spot frame to frame.

### Perspective

By tilting and spinning flat 2D layers, the CC Perspective effects let you fake 3D depth and lend them a more spatial, lively feel. Think of it as a shortcut to the illusion of depth that skips converting anything into a true 3D layer.

- CC Cylinder: Bends a layer around the surface of a 3D cylinder.
- CC Environment: Builds a reflective, spherical environment map you can lean on to fake reflections across 3D objects.
- CC Sphere: Bends a layer — an image or a video, say — into a 3D sphere.
- CC Spotlight: Casts a lighting effect that reads like a spotlight trained on a layer, lending it a 3D-ish quality.

### Simulation

Reach for the CC Simulation effects when you want lifelike touches such as storms, fire, or smoke dropped into a scene. They build a richer mood through animated elements that read as natural.

- CC Bubbles: Conjures bubbles drifting up through liquid, a great match for underwater shots or a glass of soda.
- CC Drizzle: Lays down a faint rain look, the kind of gentle drizzle that drifts over the comp.
- CC Hair: Mimics strands — hair, thread, or fiber — that sway naturally with wind or movement.
- CC Mr. Mercury: Yields a flowing, dripping liquid look reminiscent of mercury or other heavy fluids.
- CC Particle Systems II: Builds looks such as fire, smoke, explosions, or dust out of particles.
- CC Particle World: Drops in particle-driven looks where the particles roam freely through 3D space, with tunable traits like color, speed, and size.
- CC Pixel Polly: Shatters a layer into little shards or pixels so it appears to fall apart.
- CC Rainfall: Lays down falling rain, with drops descending from the top edge of the comp.
- CC Scatterize: Breaks a layer into smaller bits and flings them at random inside a set region.
- CC Snowfall: Drops a snow look, flakes drifting down from above like a winter scene.
- CC Star Burst: Fires particles out from a central point, like the opening of an explosion or an energy blast.

### Stylize

The CC Stylize effects exist to layer artistic, creative flourishes onto footage or comps. What they deliver are distinctive, non-photoreal treatments that bring drama and a singular makeover to your work.

- CC Block Load: Fakes an image loading in big, blocky chunks to build a pixelated, grid-style transition.
- CC Burn Film: Lays a retro film look over the shot with burn marks and scratches, echoing aged or worn footage.
- CC Glass: Warps the picture as if light were refracting through glass, giving a textured, bent appearance.
- CC HexTile: Chops the picture into hexagon tiles for a honeycomb-style pattern.
- CC Kaleida: Reworks footage by copying and mirroring slices into symmetrical patterns, kaleidoscope-style.
- CC Mr. Smoothie: Eases off sharp edges for a smoother, more stylized read while holding the overall shape.
- CC Plastic: Lends a glossy, shiny finish much like a plastic surface.
- CC RepeTile: Echoes the picture across a tiled grid, laying out a pattern of repeated copies.
- CC Threshold: Reduces the picture to high-contrast black and white.
- CC Threshold RGB: Works like CC Threshold but runs separately on the Red, Green, and Blue channels for a more colorful payoff.
- CC Vignette: Darkens or brightens the picture's edges to pull the viewer's eye toward the center of the comp.

### Time

With CC Time Effects you get to bend how time behaves inside a video. Tack on delays, stretch the pacing so motion runs slower or faster, or warp time outright to build trickier movement.

- CC Force Motion Blur: Lays motion blur onto a layer, blurring moving subjects so their movement reads as more lifelike, especially through quick action.
- CC Wide Time: Blends several frames together to produce a motion blur that trails off like a ghostly streak.

Pictured: the CC Wide Time effect with the Forward Steps option's value set to 124.

### Transition

In After Effects, the CC Transition effects are there to build polished, inventive hand-offs from one clip to the next. The set spans dissolves, wipes, and slides, so moving between scenes carries extra style and visual interest.

- CC Glass Wipe: Lays in a transition that mimics a streak or smudge being wiped clear, exposing the clip beneath.
- CC Grid Wipe: Lays in a transition whose grid pattern slowly uncovers the next clip or image.
- CC Image Wipe: Lays in a transition that wipes an image or texture across the screen as a mask, exposing the layer below.
- CC Jaws: Lays in a transition that mimics a chomp — a giant toothy mouth "eating" the current scene to expose the next.
- CC Light Wipe: Runs a light source sweeping over the screen, wiping the current scene away and bringing up the next.
- CC Line Sweep: Runs a single moving line across the screen that gradually uncovers the clip behind it.
- CC Radial ScaleWipe: Lays in a transition that wipes with a zoom starting from the center or an edge, scaling into the next scene.
- CC Scale Wipe: Lays in a transition that pairs a swipe with a zoom to glide smoothly into the next scene.
- CC Twister: Lays in a swirling, twisting move that distorts the current scene as it crosses into the next.
- CC WarpoMatic: Lays in a stretchy warp that bends and distorts the current scene while it morphs into the next.

### Utility

What the Utility effect hands you is an assortment of tools — fusing several layers or elements, tuning brightness and contrast, dialing in color balance, and more.

- CC Overbright: Lays on an effect that pushes the brightness of highlight regions, yielding an overexposed or glowing read while leaving midtones and shadows alone.

## Expression basics

### Pick up the fundamentals of expressions in Adobe After Effects so you can automate animation, tie properties together, and drive dynamic motion inside your comps.

Faced with building and linking elaborate animation but reluctant to hand-place dozens or hundreds of keyframes? That's where expressions earn their keep. An expression is a short snippet of JavaScript that you attach to animated properties in your After Effects projects; for any one layer property at any given moment, it resolves to a single value.

A script directs the application to carry out a task; an expression, by contrast, directs a property to do so. Picture a ball traveling across the screen from left to right that you also want to wiggle. Instead of keyframing the Position property, you can hand it a wiggle expression.

Although the expressions language sits on top of standard JavaScript, you can dive into expressions without any JavaScript know-how. Build them with the pick whip, or grab simple examples and tweak them to fit what you need. Here's a basic example of looping with expressions:

### Why use expressions?

Work faster and spin up animation in a hurry. Expressions let you put actions on autopilot — wiggle, jitter, or bounce, for instance. The payoff in time is large, since you skip cutting fresh keyframes for every action. A handful of starting points follows.

Link different properties.

Lean on expressions to bind one property to another, say Rotation and Position, across comps. That linking lets you spin up varied animation without authoring a separate expression for each.

Create motion infographics.

Expressions come in handy while you build Motion Graphics templates. Tweak assorted properties, tie animations together, and reshape the MOGRTs inside After Effects.

Control multiple layers to create complex animations.

With the pick whip you can steer many animations from just a few controls, producing results that other features would make far more laborious.

Create animated graphs and charts.

Whip up animated and motion infographics in short order — a living world map, say, or animated bar graphs charting the pollution index across countries.

Save and reuse expressions.

Stash expressions as templates and pull them into your other After Effects projects. There's no reason to reinvent the wheel!

### Expressions and Keyframes

Every property in After Effects that can be animated carries both a pre-expression value and a post-expression value.

Think of the pre-expression value as the property's value before any expression touches it — whether that value is fixed or already driven by keyframes. It parallels a layer's raw pixels with no effects on them. An expression can then read or alter those pre-expression values — much as an effect reworks or draws on a layer's pixels — and the result is the post-expression value.

You can tell which one a property is showing by the highlight color on its value in the UI: blue marks pre-expression values, red marks post-expression ones.

Inside an expression, reach a property's pre-expression value through the attribute value. Take value+90 on a Rotation property — it tacks 90 degrees onto whatever the property held before the expression ran. Keyframed values behave the same way; with Rotation keyframed from 0 to 45, that same value+90 expression drives it from 90 to 135. You can likewise read individual keyframe values via key(index).value — pass 1 as the index for the first keyframe, 2 for the second, and so on. Because the attribute numKeys always matches the count of keyframes on the property, key(numKeys).value gives you the final keyframe's value.

A handful of methods — wiggle() and the assorted loops, for instance — draw on the pre-expression value behind the scenes.

To illustrate, on a property sitting at 0 the expression wiggle(10, 10) swings between -10 and 10, layering wiggle motion around the value it already has. The behavior carries over to a keyframed property as well, stacking secondary animation atop the original animated values.

### The Expression Language menu

Living in the Timeline panel, the Expression Language menu holds After Effects–specific language elements that you can drop into an expression. Treat it as a reference for which elements are available — it's handy for confirming valid elements and getting their syntax right. Choose any object, attribute, or method from it, and After Effects drops that selection straight into the expression field at the insertion point. When text in the expression field is already selected, the inserted expression text takes its place. When the insertion point sits outside the expression field, the inserted text overwrites everything in the field.

### Common expressions you can use

### Wiggle

Wiggle randomly

Wiggle randomly

Among the After Effects expressions you'll reach for most often is wiggle. True to its name, it nudges a property around by a random amount — a handy trick for giving a scene a less rigid, more lifelike feel.

What sits inside the parentheses governs the motion: the leading value sets how many wiggles occur each second, and the trailing value sets the wiggle amount. Applied to a Position property, wiggle(2,30) therefore shakes the property twice every second across a span of up to 30 pixels.

### Time

Infinite rotating 40 degrees per second in time

Infinite rotating 40 degrees per second in time

For anything meant to keep moving without stopping, reach for the time expression. Say you need an object that spins forever: attach the expression time to its Rotation property and it turns at 1 degree per second. Because plain arithmetic works here too, multiplying it as time\*40 spins that same object forty times quicker.

### Loop

Loop in and out

Loop in and out

Stacking up keyframes by hand to build repeating motion eats up time. The loop expression handles that chore for you and can drive elaborate looping animation instead. It comes in two flavours — loopIn and loopOut, the latter being the one people lean on. Drop loopOut(); or loopOut("cycle"); onto a Rotation property and your animation keeps repeating right through to the end of the timeline.
